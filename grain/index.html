How Wood and Stone Get Their Patterns

Some patterns are not painted. A small rule makes them, and the same rule can make endless new versions.

Time 20 min
You will learn how noise makes grain and veins
Try first drag across the bowl to see the real wood
Core lesson A rule, not an image

What you will see

NoiseRandom values that change smoothly from place to place.
OctaveOne layer of noise. Each new layer is twice as fine.
fBmMany octaves added together: big shapes plus small detail.
Growth ringOne year of tree growth, seen as a band in the wood.
TurbulenceNoise that bends straight bands into folds and veins.
Solid textureA pattern that fills the whole 3D block, not only its skin.

Specimen 01 — Noise

Random, but smooth

Noise is a list of random values that blend smoothly into each other.

The top of the strip shows noise as light and dark. The graph below shows one line across it.

One octave gives only big, soft hills. Each extra octave adds hills that are half as wide and half as tall.

Added together, the octaves give big shapes with fine detail. This sum is called fBm.

Try this

Set Octaves to 1 and look at the graph. Then raise it to 6, one step at a time. The big hills stay, and small bumps appear on top of them.

Specimen 02 — Wood

Rings around a trunk

A tree grows one new ring each year. Light wood grows in spring, and darker wood grows in late summer.

The rule here draws circles around a line, like rings around a trunk.

Noise pushes the circles sideways, so no two rings are the same.

On the end of the board you see the rings. On the long side, the same rings stretch out into lines and arches called grain.

Try this

Move Turn to see the end of the board, then the long side. Set Warp to 0 and the rings become perfect circles, which look fake. Raise Warp until they look real.

Specimen 03 — Marble

Bands bent by heat and pressure

Marble starts as limestone. Deep underground, heat and pressure change it into new crystals.

Over a very long time, the rock can bend and fold without breaking. Layers that were flat become curved bands.

Small amounts of other minerals, such as clay or iron oxide, give the bands and veins their color. Some veins form where cracks fill with new minerals.

The rule here starts with straight bands. Turbulence is noise that bends them, like the folding in real rock.

Try this

Set Turbulence to 0. You see straight, flat layers. Raise it slowly and watch the layers fold into veins. Then press Gold veins to change the mineral color.

Specimen 04 — Cut the block

The inside matches the outside

Left: the wood comes from a 3D rule. The rule gives a color to every point inside the block, not only to its skin.

Right: a 2D picture of the same wood covers only the skin, like a sticker.

From the outside, the two blocks look the same.

Cut the left block, and the new face shows rings that join the grain on the sides. Cut the right block, and it is empty. A 2D picture has no inside, so there is nothing to show.

Try this

Drag Cut slowly. On the left, follow one dark ring from the side of the block onto the cut face. Then look into the right block. What is inside it?

Where you see this

Real people in real jobs use rules like these to make wood and stone, or they read the real patterns in nature.

  • Game worlds

    Technical artist

    A technical artist builds procedural materials that make rock, sand, and bark from rules instead of one painted image. The noise does not repeat in an obvious way, so one material can cover a huge game world without a visible grid.

  • Film visual effects

    FX artist

    An FX artist sets up objects that break, crack, or get carved in a shot. When wood, rock, or marble use a solid 3D pattern, every new broken face shows matching grain or veins, so the inside looks as real as the outside.

  • Architecture

    Architectural visualization artist

    An architectural visualization artist puts wood floors and marble counters into pictures of rooms that are not built yet. Variation from noise gives each board and slab its own grain, so the floor does not look like one board copied many times.

  • Furniture making

    Furniture designer

    A furniture designer chooses how each board is cut from the log and which way the grain runs. The cut decides the figure, such as straight lines on quartersawn boards or arches on flatsawn boards, and wood is much stronger along the grain than across it.

  • Earth science

    Geologist

    A geologist reads the bands and veins in marble to learn the history of the rock. Folded bands show that the rock bent under heat and pressure deep underground, and veins show where cracks opened and new minerals grew inside them.

For teachers

Short objectives, one classroom loop, and how the lab maps to studio practice, wood science, and geology.

Learning objectives

  • Describe noise as random values that change smoothly, and octaves as layers of finer detail.
  • Explain why wood shows rings on the end of a board and lines or arches on its long side.
  • Explain how heat, pressure, and minerals give marble its folded bands and veins.
  • Explain why a 3D pattern matches on a cut face and a 2D picture does not.

10-minute classroom activity

1) Give each pair a wooden pencil or a scrap of wood, and a photo of a marble surface. 2) Students sketch the end and the long side of the wood, and mark one ring on both. 3) They draw arrows on the marble photo to show where the bands fold. 4) Finish on Specimen 04: cut both blocks and write one sentence on why only one cut face matches.

Art & design map

Procedural materials for games and film, wood and stone in interior visualization, grain direction and figure in furniture, faux marble and wood graining in decorative painting.

Science map

Tree growth rings and dating by rings, earlywood and latewood, anisotropy (wood is stronger along the grain), metamorphic rock, folding under heat and pressure, and mineral veins in cracks.
