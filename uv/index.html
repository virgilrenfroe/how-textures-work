How Maps Wrap a Shape

A painted image is flat. A 3D model is not. UV maps cut and unfold the surface so the image can stick — and seams and stretch show where that unfold goes wrong.

UV net · cylinder

Specimen 01 — UV maps

Cut flat, wrap back on

A painted image lives on a flat square. A cylinder is a curved surface. To stick the image on, you cut the surface and unfold it into that square. That unfold is the UV map.

Good UVs keep squares looking square on the model. Stretched UVs make the checker look pulled or cramped.

A seam is where the cut edges meet again. On a cylinder the seam is a vertical line. If the pattern does not match across it, you see a break.

Try this

Press Good UVs and look at the checker. Then press Stretched and watch the squares pull. Press Show seam to mark the cut. Turn on Show UV net to see the flat rectangle that wraps this cylinder.

Specimen 02 — Seams

Where the cut meets again

When you unfold a surface, you cut it. On the model those cut edges sit next to each other again. That join is the seam.

If the colors on each side of the seam do not line up, the pattern breaks. Artists hide seams in places that are hard to see, or they paint across the join so the break disappears.

Here the stripes should continue around the cylinder. Move the seam offset and you make the break yourself.

Try this

Set Seam offset to 0. The stripes meet cleanly. Raise it until you see a clear break. Then bring it back to 0 to fix the join.

Specimen 03 — Triplanar

Project from three sides

Left: the rock uses a UV unwrap. Complex shapes need careful cuts, and bad UVs leave stretch or seams.

Right: triplanar mapping projects the same image from the X, Y, and Z axes, then blends the three results by the surface normal. It needs no UV map.

Triplanar hides seams on rocks, terrain, and modular pieces. On sharp corners the blend can look soft or blurred. Blend sharpness controls how quickly one projection wins over the others.

Try this

Turn both rocks. Look for stretch or a seam on the left. On the right, lower Blend sharpness until the corners look smeared, then raise it until the faces look crisp.

Specimen 04 — Texel density

How many pixels per unit

Texel density is how many texture pixels cover each unit of surface. If the UV island is too small, the checker looks large and soft. If it is too big, the checker looks fine, but you waste texture space.

Film and game teams match texel density across props so a close-up does not look blurrier than the rest of the shot.

Try this

Move UV scale down until the checker looks soft and blocky. Then raise it until the squares look sharp. Watch the texels-per-unit number change.

Where you see this

People who paint maps, build materials, and dress large worlds use UVs and triplanar projection every day.

  • Character and prop art

    Texture artist

    A texture artist lays out UV islands for characters and props so painted detail lands where it should. They hide seams under clothing or along hard edges, and they keep island scale even so a face does not look blurrier than a boot.

  • Games and real-time worlds

    Technical artist

    A technical artist builds triplanar materials for terrain and modular kits. Rocks and walls can share one material without a unique unwrap on every piece, which saves layout time and keeps seams from showing on kit-bashed sets.

  • Open-world environments

    Environment artist

    An environment artist covers large worlds with rock, dirt, and moss. Triplanar and world-space projection let them dress cliffs and rubble without painting unique UVs for every boulder, while still matching the look of hand-unwrapped hero props nearby.

  • Film look development

    Look-dev artist

    A look-dev artist checks texel density and seam placement in close-ups. If a hero prop sits nearer the camera than the set around it, they raise its UV scale or texture resolution so the grain and print stay sharp when the lens moves in.

  • Product and packaging

    Product visualization artist

    A product visualization artist unwraps packaging and labels so logos and small text stay readable on bottles, boxes, and cans. Straight type needs undistorted UVs; a stretched island would pull the letters and make the brand look wrong.

For teachers

Short objectives, one classroom loop, and how the lab maps to studio practice and spatial reasoning.

Learning objectives

  • Explain why a flat image needs a UV unwrap to stick to a 3D surface.
  • Identify a seam and describe how a misaligned pattern creates a visible break.
  • Describe stretch as UV islands that are too large or too small for the surface.
  • Explain how triplanar projection blends X, Y, and Z projections without UVs, and name one trade-off.
  • Relate texel density to how sharp a map looks on a given surface size.

10-minute classroom activity

1) Hand out a paper label and a cardboard tube. 2) Students wrap the label, mark the join, and unfold it flat — that flat is a UV net. 3) On Specimen 02 they misalign the seam, then fix it. 4) On Specimen 03 they write one sentence on when they would choose triplanar over a hand unwrap.
