How Stickers Sit on Top

A base material covers the whole object. A decal is a smaller image — a logo, stain, crack, or number — that sits on top in one place, without re-cutting the unwrap.

Time 20 min
You will learn decals, opacity, and projection
Try first drag across the crate to see the real sticker under the dots
Core lesson One base, many stickers

Enter the lab ↓

What you will see

Base materialThe map or look that covers the whole object.
DecalA smaller image that sits on top in one place, like a sticker.
ProjectionPressing the sticker onto the surface from a direction.
OpacityHow strongly the sticker covers what is underneath.
BlendWhether the sticker replaces the base or mixes with it.
StackTwo or more stickers in order, such as a scratch over a logo.

Specimen 01 — Place the sticker

Move it without a new unwrap

The crate uses one base material for wood and metal. The logo is a separate image pressed onto the front face.

You do not re-cut the UV map of the whole crate. You only place the sticker: slide it, scale it, and turn it.

Games and film use this so one crate material can become many unique props with different numbers and marks.

Try this

Move Offset X and Offset Y until the logo sits in a corner. Raise Scale, then turn Rotation. The base wood stays the same while only the sticker moves.

Specimen 02 — Opacity and blend

Cover, or let the base show through

A solid logo often covers the base completely. Dirt and soft stains usually mix with what is underneath so metal and wood still read.

Opacity sets how strong the sticker is. At zero you see only the base. At one the sticker is at full strength.

Cover mode replaces the base where the sticker is opaque. Multiply mode darkens the base, like a translucent stain.

Try this

Set Opacity low, then raise it. Press Cover, then Multiply. Watch whether the metal under the stain disappears or stays visible.

Specimen 03 — Projection angle

A steep aim stretches the sticker

A projected decal is like holding a sticker to the surface from one direction. When the aim is square to the face, the logo looks true.

When the aim is steep, the same sticker stretches across the side — foreshortening. Artists aim projectors carefully so labels stay readable.

The thin magenta arrow shows the projection direction.

Try this

Set Angle near 0 and read the number. Raise Angle toward 70 and watch the sticker stretch down the side of the box.

Aim · square to the face

Specimen 04 — Stack

Scratch over logo

Decals can stack. The order matters: a scratch drawn after a logo sits on top of the logo and cuts through it.

In plain terms, the last sticker painted wins where they overlap. That is draw order.

One base crate plus a logo layer and a damage layer can sell a unique prop without a new unwrap.

Try this

Turn Logo off, then on. Turn Scratch off, then on. With both on, look where the scratch crosses the logo — the scratch sits on top.

Draw order · base → logo → scratch

Where you see this

People who dress levels and props use decals so one base material can carry many stickers, stains, and marks.

  • Game levels

    Environment artist

    An environment artist stamps damage, posters, and graffiti into game levels. One wall or crate material becomes many unique surfaces without painting a new unwrap for every mark.

  • Runtime damage systems

    Technical artist

    A technical artist builds decal systems that spawn bullet holes, scuffs, and burns while the game runs. The system projects a small image onto the hit surface so damage appears where the shot landed.

  • Film props

    Look-dev artist

    A look-dev artist adds label and dirt layers on film props so a clean hero asset can read as used, shipped, or battle-worn in close-ups without rebuilding the base material.

  • Packaging and product

    Product visualization artist

    A product visualization artist places logos and regulatory marks on packaging so type stays sharp and correctly aimed on bottles, boxes, and cans in catalog shots.

  • Automotive CGI

    Automotive CGI artist

    An automotive CGI artist lays racing numbers, sponsor badges, and team marks onto car bodies. Decals keep the paint material shared while each car wears a unique set of stickers.

For teachers

Short objectives, one classroom loop, and how the lab maps to studio layering practice.

Learning objectives

  • Explain why a decal can mark one place without re-unwrapping the whole model.
  • Place a sticker with offset, scale, and rotation controls.
  • Contrast cover opacity with a multiply (stain) blend in plain language.
  • Describe how a steep projection stretches a sticker on a side face.
  • State that stacked stickers follow draw order (last on top).

10-minute classroom activity

1) Give pairs a cardboard box and sticky notes as “decals.” 2) Students place a number, then a torn strip over it — note which sits on top. 3) On Specimen 03, raise Angle and sketch the stretched logo. 4) Write one sentence on when they would use a multiply stain instead of a solid logo.

Art & design map

Level dressing with posters and damage, runtime hit decals, film prop weathering layers, packaging logos, racing numbers and badges.

Science map

Projection onto surfaces, foreshortening, opacity as coverage, multiply as filtering light through a stain, and ordered layer compositing.
